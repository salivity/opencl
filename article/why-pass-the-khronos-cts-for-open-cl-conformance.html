Why Pass the Khronos CTS for OpenCL Conformance

Hardware vendors must successfully pass the Khronos Conformance Test Suite (CTS) before marketing their products as OpenCL conformant due to strict legal, trademark, and technical interoperability requirements. OpenCL is not merely an open standard; it is a legally protected trademark owned by Apple Inc. and administered by the Khronos Group. This article explains how the CTS acts as the gatekeeper for intellectual property rights, API portability, and legal protection against trademark infringement.

Trademark Law and the Adopters Agreement

The name "OpenCL" and the associated OpenCL logo are registered trademarks. To use these marks commercially—such as on packaging, spec sheets, marketing collateral, or runtime drivers—a hardware vendor must execute a Khronos OpenCL Adopters Agreement.

Under this binding contract, the trademark license is conditional. The agreement explicitly stipulates that the right to claim OpenCL support or display the logo is only granted once an implementation passes the designated CTS for that specific API version and is officially accepted by the Khronos Working Group. Marketing a non-certified implementation violates this contract and constitutes trademark infringement.

Ensuring Cross-Platform Portability

The primary technical goal of OpenCL is to enable cross-platform, heterogeneous parallel computing across CPUs, GPUs, DSPs, and FPGAs. If vendors could claim OpenCL support without verification, minor deviations from the specification would cause software to fail across different hardware.

The Khronos CTS consists of thousands of automated tests that verify:

  • Exact mathematical precision and rounding modes.
  • Proper execution of the OpenCL C and SPIR-V specifications.
  • Memory model consistency and synchronization primitives.
  • Complete and correct implementation of all required core API entry points.

By making CTS passage mandatory, Khronos prevents fragmentation, ensuring that developers can rely on consistent behavior regardless of the underlying silicon.

Patent Indemnification and Intellectual Property

Beyond trademark usage, CTS validation unlocks vital intellectual property protections. Khronos operates under a defined intellectual property framework where participating members grant reciprocal, royalty-free patent licenses covering the specification.

This patent indemnity, often referred to as a "safe harbor," applies exclusively to conformant implementations. If a vendor distributes a non-conformant implementation, they—and potentially their customers—forfeit these cross-licensing protections, exposing themselves to patent litigation from other member companies.

The Formal Review Process

Passing the CTS locally is not enough. To legally finalize conformance, vendors must complete a structured submission process:

  1. Test Execution: The vendor runs the full CTS suite on their designated hardware and operating system, generating complete, unaltered test logs.
  2. Confidential Submission: The results and runtime configurations are submitted to the Khronos OpenCL Working Group for formal review.
  3. Peer Review: Working group members have a mandatory 30-day window to scrutinize the test results, identify potential discrepancies, and raise challenges.
  4. Official Listing: If no issues are found, or once all challenges are resolved, Khronos registers the product on the official Khronos Conformant Products list.

Only after the product is officially listed can the vendor legally state that their hardware or driver conforms to the OpenCL standard.
