Numerical Stability in OpenCL PDE Solvers

Executing partial differential equation (PDE) solvers in OpenCL allows massive parallelization across GPUs and accelerators, but the underlying hardware architecture introduces unique numerical stability challenges. This article examines the core factors affecting numerical stability when porting PDE solvers to OpenCL, including floating-point precision, compiler-level optimizations, non-associative parallel reductions, time-step constraints, and synchronization barriers.

Floating-Point Precision Constraints

Most OpenCL devices (particularly consumer GPUs) optimize heavily for single-precision (32-bit float) calculations, often executing double-precision (64-bit float) operations at a fraction of the speed. However, using 32-bit floats for iterative PDE solvers frequently results in rapid accumulation of truncation errors and catastrophic cancellation, particularly in high-order spatial stencils or stiff differential equations.

To maintain stability:

  • Verify device capabilities for the cl_khr_fp64 extension before deploying solvers sensitive to round-off drift.
  • If limited to single precision, implement compensated summation algorithms (such as Kahan summation) or use mixed-precision strategies where critical spatial derivatives and updates are accumulated with higher precision.

Compiler Flags and Fast-Math Deviations

OpenCL compilers provide aggressive optimization flags, such as -cl-fast-relaxed-math, -cl-mad-enable, and -cl-finite-math-only. While these significantly improve execution throughput, they sacrifice strict IEEE 754 compliance:

  • Division and square root operations are replaced with low-accuracy approximations.
  • Fused Multiply-Add (FMA) instructions may be generated unpredictably, altering rounding behavior compared to baseline CPU models.
  • Flush-To-Zero (FTZ) modes may be enabled by default, setting subnormal numbers to zero and corrupting operations dependent on minute gradient transitions.

Disable relaxed-math flags for numerical kernels where solver convergence relies on exact boundary gradients and strict monotonicity.

Non-Associative Reductions and Determinism

Floating-point addition is mathematically non-associative: (a + b) + c ≠ a + (b + c). In parallel PDE solvers, computing global properties—such as the maximum wave speed for adaptive time-stepping or the global residual in iterative linear solvers (e.g., Conjugate Gradient)—requires parallel reduction operations across work-items and work-groups.

Because the execution order of work-groups depends on dynamic GPU scheduling, rounding errors vary between runs. This non-determinism can alter step sizes or cause erratic convergence paths in highly non-linear PDEs. Implement deterministic reduction trees or fixed-order hierarchical reductions to maintain reproducibility and stable step-size evaluation.

Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) Condition Enforcement

Explicit PDE schemes require strict adherence to the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability criterion, where the time step (\(\Delta t\)) must remain below a threshold dictated by spatial grid spacing (\(\Delta x\)) and maximum characteristic propagation speed.

In OpenCL:

  • Calculating dynamic \(\Delta t\) requires computing the global maximum speed across all grid cells at each step.
  • Because synchronizing across the entire grid requires either multiple kernel dispatches or device-wide atomics, developers are often tempted to update \(\Delta t\) infrequently to avoid launch latency.
  • Infrequent recalculation of the CFL limit can allow localized shocks or fluid instabilities to exceed the stability envelope before the time step adjusts, causing solver explosion.

Ghost Cell Synchronization and Memory Barriers

Domain decomposition across work-groups relies on halo exchanges (ghost cells) stored in OpenCL local memory (__local) or global memory:

  • Work-Group Level: Stencil computations reading adjacent cells from local memory require explicit barriers using barrier(CLK_LOCAL_MEM_FENCE). Omitting a barrier or placing it inside non-uniform conditional branches creates race conditions, leading to stale or corrupted halo reads that destabilize the grid interface.
  • Global Grid Level: When the PDE grid spans multiple work-groups, inter-work-group communication cannot be safely synchronized within a single kernel invocation. Splitting iterations into distinct kernel executions ensures that all halo exchanges are globally committed before the next spatial integration step begins.
